The Fabriano papermakers guarded their wire designs closely, because a watermark was as much a mark of origin as a mark of quality. A sheet bearing a known device could command a better price in Venice or Florence, and disputes over imitation designs reached the guild courts more than once. By the fifteenth century the practice had spread along the trade routes into France and the German states, and paper mills began dating their moulds, which is why watermarks are now one of the most useful dating tools available to historians of early printing. A scholar examining an undated pamphlet will often photograph the sheet over a light source, trace the wire design, and compare it against catalogues of known moulds to place the paper within a decade and sometimes within a single mill.

The security printing industry took the idea much further. When governments began issuing paper currency at scale in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the watermark became a first line of defence against forgery, precisely because it is formed in the substance of the paper rather than printed on its surface. A counterfeiter working with ordinary stock cannot add one after the fact. Modern banknote paper builds portraits and denomination numerals into the sheet using electrotype and cylinder mould techniques, and cashiers are still trained to hold a note to the light before anything else.

Photography and cinema borrowed the vocabulary but changed the mechanism. Studio proofs carried visible stamps to spoil unauthorised reproduction, and television channels later adopted persistent corner logos for the same reason. The digital turn in the 1990s made the problem harder and the solutions stranger. A digital file can be copied perfectly, so researchers began hiding identifying signals inside the content itself, spreading tiny perturbations across the pixels of an image or the samples of an audio track. Done well, the change is imperceptible to a viewer but recoverable by a decoder that knows where to look, and it survives resizing, recompression, and format conversion to a surprising degree.

Broadcast monitoring firms used such marks to verify that advertisements actually aired. Film studios embedded serial numbers in review copies so that leaked screeners could be traced to a recipient. Photo agencies marked their previews so that a cropped and re-saved image could still be identified in the wild. Each application shares the same underlying bargain: accept a minute, controlled distortion of the content in exchange for a durable, checkable claim about its origin. The bargain has limits, and every scheme eventually meets an adversary willing to degrade the content enough to scrub the signal, at which point the question becomes whether the surviving copy is still worth having.
